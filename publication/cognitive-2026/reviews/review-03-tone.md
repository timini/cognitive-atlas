## Editorial diagnosis

The research is interesting enough to develop, but the present paper is not yet a strong cognitive-science submission. Its main weakness is not pompous prose or exaggerated claims. It is an argument that steadily retreats from a cognitive question into a careful benchmark report. That retreat is understandable after methods review, but it no longer expresses the user's intended contribution.

The opening sentence, “What geographic configuration best reconciles a language model's answers about distances between places?”, is a useful measurement question. The next paragraph's distinction among accuracy, consistency and variation is a real strength. It should become the entry point to a cognitive problem: under what conditions do separately elicited spatial judgments behave as a shared relational structure?

Instead, the contribution paragraph announces “a global repeated-judgment benchmark,” and the abstract concludes that “The benchmark connects accuracy, coherence and repeated-response variation.” These sentences specify a dataset and analysis combination. They do not tell a cognitive reader what explanatory uncertainty motivates the experiment. The conclusion returns to “a reproducible account of elicited geography,” so the reader finishes where the engineering project began.

The sentence “a cognitive map is an operational description of observable spatial judgments” is appropriately modest. Keep its distinction from neural-mechanistic claims. But follow it with an explicit account of evidence: coherence, stability and transfer are separable properties, and a fitted configuration can summarize outputs without being the representation the system uses. The current next sentence leaves memorized facts, heuristics and computation compatible; this is accurate, but the paper does not explain how a cognitive research program can start separating them.

The Discussion is unusually honest about its evidence. It correctly says the sign control concerns arrangement, does not assign a neural or cultural mechanism, and explains why statistical detectability need not create visually different world maps. Preserve those passages. However, its dominant progression is result, caveat, result, caveat, limitations, future work. It needs a positive conclusion about what is established before carefully bounding it: the elicited relations have highly ordered global structure, yet aggregate accuracy alone conceals differences in signed organization and repeated-response behavior. Those are properties of a behavioral measurement, not proof of an internal world model.

## Psychogeography and the intended contribution

The user's interest is legitimate: how places are arranged in the spatial world expressed by a language system, and how that arrangement changes with context. Do not erase this merely because the system has no demonstrated subjective experience. Describe the project as elicited cognitive geography or behavioral spatial representation and explain precisely what the measurements capture.

At the same time, this dataset contains numeric great-circle judgments. It does not measure affective meanings, attachment to places, lived urban experience or human navigation. A psychogeographic motivation can introduce questions about the arrangement and salience of places, but the paper cannot claim to have measured those additional constructs. The title alone cannot bridge that gap. Human comparisons are useful for claims about human-like distortions; neural measurements are useful for claims about implementation. Neither is a universal prerequisite to an honestly bounded behavioral study of an artificial system.

## A better argumentative structure — proposed, not implemented

1. Open with the distinction between having accurate spatial facts and expressing a coherent relational organization. Explain why language is an informative acquisition channel for testing this distinction.
2. Define the inferential object as a geometry fitted to observable judgments. Separate observed judgments, the fitted measurement model and the hypothesized generating process.
3. State three present questions: how much true distance ordering is preserved; what organization remains after controlling absolute error; and how much the observed structure varies across repeated requests and prompt conditions. Label them exploratory.
4. Position the prior language-derived and prompted-distance reconstructions as precedents. Explain the incremental advance of repeated sampling, global arc geometry and matched-error diagnostics without a first-discovery claim.
5. Present results in that conceptual order. Use geography to calibrate the instrument; use maps to explain the relationships. Keep the raw-versus-fit and local-neighborhood tradeoff visible.
6. Discuss what is constrained and what is not. The current data support a stable-looking aggregate structure in the tested sample, but do not establish stability across sessions, a unique hidden map, an isolated language effect or task-general relational use.
7. End with a small discriminating next experiment, rather than a list of models and software capabilities. Move purely operational audit material out of the main narrative; retain caveats that alter substantive interpretation.

A possible working title is **“The Geometry of Elicited Spatial Knowledge: Repeated Language-Model Judgments across 100 Capitals.”** This is an editorial suggestion, not a revised artifact or a claim of measured neural geometry.

## What is currently interesting, and what more would matter?

The strongest current insight is not that a model's Earth is dramatically warped. Rank accuracy is very high and the unamplified maps are visually similar. The useful observation is that good pairwise accuracy does not uniquely characterize the organization of errors: English and Chinese lie below the descriptive independent-sign control ranges, while Arabic lies above its range. That pattern motivates a question about structured judgments. It does not validate that artificial control as a sampling null or identify a cognitive mechanism.

The prompt-condition shifts are measurable but do not demonstrate language-specific latent worlds. The permutation tests concern response distributions under exchangeability assumptions; dispersion can contribute, instructions differ in wording as well as language, and requests were tightly clustered. Regional point estimates do not show an absolute local-language advantage. These limits must remain in any cognitive version.

Adding other model families would be useful. A repeat on two independently developed families could show whether a distortion signature is shared or model-specific. Merely adding two versions from the same family would answer a narrower robustness question. A brand-by-language table of MAEs, however, would preserve the present explanation gap.

The more informative addition is a prespecified cross-task test: use numeric judgments to predict new triplet choices or directional relations and compare those predictions with simple factual or geographic-heuristic baselines. The numeric fit must not use the held-out outcomes. Pair this with independently checked translations, multiple prompt formulations and separated randomized collection blocks. These changes test whether an inferred configuration has predictive value beyond the questions used to construct it. Different tasks can themselves introduce task demands, so transfer is evidence to weigh, not automatic proof of a shared hidden map.

For human-cognition venues, a matched human judgment comparison or a tested cognitive model would sharpen what the machine data teach about cognition. For artificial-cognition-inclusive venues, a carefully motivated behavioral transfer study can be valuable without claiming human equivalence. My first practical target is Cognitive Processing after substantive revision; Spatial Cognition & Computation is highly relevant but has a verified AI-drafting eligibility obstacle. More ambitious venues need a sharper theoretical result, not just more API calls.
